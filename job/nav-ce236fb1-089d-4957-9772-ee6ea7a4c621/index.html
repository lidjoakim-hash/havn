About the project Globally, electricity demand is increasing and expected to increase in the coming years, supported by higher consumption from industrial actors, increasing electrification and integration of electric vehicles, data centers and other electrical sources. The electrical grid is getting increasingly strained due to the increased load, which needs to handle the power-intense appliances, while also needing to enable more renewable energy production into the power system. In both the EU and Norway, the ageing distribution grid is expected to have challenges in the future year due to the energy transition, needing investments and reinforcements to prevent critical situations such as voltage issues and bottlenecks in the grid. To combat these issues due to long lead time on investments and immature replacement situations, flexibility is seen as a vital part to enable more active operation of the electrical grid to prolong secure operational conditions. Ensuring local flexible resources in local distributional grids can be enabled for grid support and be integrated as part of the operational solutions without impacting their own operational situations to a large degree will be key to understanding and assessing the potential available.  The objective of this PhD project is to develop new knowledge, models, methods, and analytical insights into how flexible resources can be utilized to provide grid support to the local distribution grid, while also participating in defined electrical markets and balancing markets. The flexible resources aim to maximize their revenues through different market options, and the consequences this will have on the local distribution grid. How upholding local conditions can be performed will be important to enable value-stacking grid services in an efficient manner. Local distributional systems operators (DSOs) might not have defined specific local markets for grid support, and how this service impacts other revenue streams and how TSO-DSO-coordination impacts operations will need to be analyzed. The project will focus on how these local conditions can be accounted for in long-term operational planning by the flexible provider, while also being accounted for during short-term operational decisions and reserve market activation sequences. Use of both active and reactive power to enable efficient grid operations will be a key aspect to approach this work. The PhD candidate will develop and apply optimization-based models that capture the dynamics of the electricity grid and different flexible providers in the distributional grid (for instance, but not limited to batteries, EV parking lots, industrial demand, etc.). Key focus will be put into methods that can simplify grid information, to reduce the need for grid-sensitive information during operational planning and operation. Regulatory aspects and TSO-DSO coordination schemes will also need to be addressed and investigated as part of the project.  The PhD candidate will be hosted by the Electricity Markets and Energy Systems Planning (EMESP) group in the Department of Electric Energy at NTNU. The main supervisor will be Associate Professor Kasper Emil Thorvaldsen, with co-supervision from relevant experts in power system operation and optimization, depending on the final methodological focus of the PhD project.
