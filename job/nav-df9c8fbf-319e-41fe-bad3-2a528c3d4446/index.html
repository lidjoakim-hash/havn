Western Norway University of Applied Sciences (HVL) has a vacancy for a PhD Research Fellow in maps and models for nature in land-use planning About the position: The PhD Research Fellow will join the Landscape, Ecology and Climate research group at the Department of Civil Engineering and Environmental Sciences, at the Sogndal campus. The position is funded through the national research center Landfutures, as part of its research area &#34;Maps and models&#34;. The position is a fixed-term, four year position with 25 % career-enhancing work at the department. The candidate will be enrolled in the PhD program Responsible Innovation and Regional Development at HVL.  Planned start date is Q1 of 2027. About the PhD project/ work tasks: About Landfutures Landfutures is an eight-year research center for sustainable land and nature use (2026– 2034), funded by the Research Council of Norway. The center is led by Western Norway Research Institute and run jointly with Western Norway University of Applied Sciences, the Norwegian Mapping Authority (Kartverket), the Institute of Transport Economics, Nordland Research Institute, Ruralis, the Norwegian Computing Center and the Basque Center for Climate Change. Landfutures focuses on interests, values and practices in planning, with special attention to the regional level, and works closely with county authorities, county governors and municipalities. Background and research need Land-use change is the main driver of biodiversity loss in Norway, and most decisions about land use outside protected areas are made in municipal land-use planning. At the core of these decisions is usually a map: which areas are most valuable for biodiversity, and where would development do the least harm? Yet mapping of nature is patchy, and although new nationwide data products such as the planned &#34;Naturkart&#34; are on the way, data alone do not tell a planner what to do. Municipalities can calculate the greenhouse-gas emissions of land-use change, but biodiversity has no currency comparable to tonnes of carbon dioxide Project-level methods such as &#34;Naturkompasset&#34; are emerging, but no spatially explicit tool supports strategic, plan-level choices about where to build. Systematic conservation planning offers  principles for such a tool: explicit targets, complementary areas, minimum cost and transparent trade-offs. Mature software exists (e.g. prioritizr, Zonation, Marxan), but the approach is hardly used in Norwegian planning. Different targets, data and methods can also point to different places on the map, and how far professionals agree on where nature matters most is an open question.  Possible research directions The PhD project addresses one overarching question: How can maps and models give robust, transparent recommendations that professionals can agree on, about where to build and where to safeguard nature? We see three broad areas in which the thesis can contribute. The examples are meant to show the range of possibilities, not a fixed work plan.  1. Practice – how plans and assessment methods treat nature today. What do existing planning documents and assessment methods actually map, prioritize and assume? Examples: how municipal biodiversity plans (&#34;kommunedelplaner for naturmangfold&#34;) reflect principles from conservation biology and landscape ecology, such as complementarity and connectivity; or how new methods for assessing nature loss in development projects, such as &#34;Naturkompasset&#34;, are structured and used. 2. Tools – spatial prioritization and how sensitive the answers are. Which planning needs can be formulated as spatial optimization problems, and how robust are the resulting recommendations? Examples: using systematic conservation planning at county scale to support plan revision (&#34;planvask&#34; – reviewing outdated zoning for development) or coordination across municipal borders; testing how much priority maps or impact scores change with different input data, criteria or assumptions about ecosystem condition. 3. Uncertainty and agreement – where experts and data diverge, and where new knowledge pays off. How large is professional disagreement about priority areas, and where would better data change the decision? Examples: how similar the priority maps are when independent groups of experts solve the same planning task; or identifying locations where additional field surveys in environmental impact assessments would be most likely to change a planning decision.  The thesis will be article-based, comprising 3–4 scientific papers. Their exact scope, cases and methods will be developed together with the supervisors, and we expect the candidate to shape at least one of them from their own ideas (see Application below). User partners in Landfutures offer opportunities to work with real planning processes and cases.  Research environment The PhD Research Fellow will be part of the Landscape, Ecology and Climate research group in Sogndal, an interdisciplinary group of ecologists and social scientists. The supervision team is Associate Professor Julien Vollering and Professor Inger Auestad, with the opportunity to involve additional supervisors where needed. Through Landfutures, the candidate will work with research partners such as the Norwegian Mapping Authority and the Norwegian Computing Center. User partners include seven county authorities, four county governors, and municipalities through the Norwegian Association of Local and Regional Authorities (KS). The center is recruiting four PhD candidates and three postdocs with different disciplinary backgrounds, and there are opportunities for exchange with complementary PhD projects within and outside Landfutures. The research fellow will recieve a fixed annual stipend to cover necessary expenses, such as participation in conferences/workshops/training or purchase of services/licenses/equipment.
